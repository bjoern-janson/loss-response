# Modern-environment extension

**Status:** extension hypothesis. Not part of the core LR-0 → LR-5 result ladder.

## Question

Could a control system adapted for **episodic adverse conditions** become chronically engaged when a technological environment repeatedly supplies signals of:

- unfavorable comparison;
- competition;
- replaceability;
- outcome volatility;
- low controllability;
- weak or unstable coupling between effort and durable reward?

The conjecture is not that "the internet causes depression" or that "AI replacement causes depression."

It is narrower:

> **Modern digital and economic environments may increase the frequency, scale, persistence, or perceived uncontrollability of inputs that would rationally push a loss-responsive controller toward conservative behavior.**

If such a controller exists, this creates a candidate evolutionary-mismatch problem.

## Candidate input shift

A rough environmental transformation is:

\[
\text{modern environment}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{comparison scale}\uparrow\\
\text{competitive set}\uparrow\\
\text{perceived replaceability}\uparrow\\
\text{outcome volatility}\uparrow\\
\text{perceived controllability}\downarrow\\
P(\text{durable reward}\mid\text{effort})\downarrow\;?
\end{cases}
\]

The final term is explicitly conditional and may differ greatly across people, occupations, and environments.

The important target is **perceived action-outcome structure**, not a blanket claim that objective opportunity is declining.

## Three empirically grounded inputs

### 1. Expanded upward comparison

Online environments expose people to unusually large pools of selectively visible comparison targets.

Two 2026 meta-analyses find robust associations between online upward social comparison and poorer psychological outcomes. One preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized 149 observational studies and 77 experiments; experimentally induced upward comparison produced short-term adverse affective effects.

This supports the existence of a comparison input. It does not establish the loss-response mechanism proposed here.

### 2. Globalized competition

Some digital labor markets make competition geographically global.

Brinatti et al. studied a large web-based remote-work platform in which workers around the world compete for the same work. Remote wages were sensitive to changes in the wages of foreign competitors.

This does not imply that all internet-mediated work becomes globally substitutable. It establishes one concrete mechanism by which the competitive reference set can expand.

### 3. Technology-induced job insecurity

Fear of replacement by automation is already measurable as a distinct workplace stressor.

Liang & Wong (2026) synthesized 96 studies involving 43,104 people. Technology-induced job insecurity was associated with strain, reduced long-term affective well-being, and more withdrawal and resistance behavior, with substantial heterogeneity and important moderators.

More generally, prospective evidence predating the current AI wave links perceived job insecurity to later depressive symptoms.

Again, perceived replacement risk is not equivalent to actual replacement.

The ILO's 2025 global exposure analysis estimates that about one in four workers are in occupations with some degree of GenAI exposure, while emphasizing that transformation of jobs is more likely than wholesale redundancy for most occupations.

## Candidate mismatch

Suppose a biological controller was useful under environments where a signal such as

\[
\text{high effort}
\rightarrow
\text{low / dangerous / uncontrollable return}
\]

usually indicated a **temporarily unpropitious local condition**.

A conservative response could then be useful:

\[
\text{exploration}\downarrow,\quad
\text{risk}\downarrow,\quad
\text{expenditure}\downarrow,\quad
\text{reliable local reward}\uparrow.
\]

The modern mismatch hypothesis is that technological environments can make relevant signals unusually:

- frequent;
- global rather than local;
- difficult to escape;
- socially visible;
- continuously updated;
- partly outside individual control.

Then a controller calibrated for episodic adversity could receive something closer to chronic input.

\[
\boxed{
\text{episodically adaptive controller}
+
\text{chronically adverse input distribution}
\rightarrow
\text{persistent conservative regime}
}
\]

This is a hypothesis, not an established description of contemporary mental health.

## The "potatoes" extension

A second, weaker hypothesis concerns the availability of low-cost, predictable rewards.

Digital environments often offer actions with some combination of:

- immediate feedback;
- low entry cost;
- low social risk;
- high repeatability;
- relatively predictable reward.

Games, feeds, streaming, and other digital activities differ enormously and should not be collapsed into one category.

The conjectural control-theoretic question is simply:

> If ambitious actions are estimated as high-cost / low-control while alternative actions provide small reliable returns, does a loss-responsive agent increasingly allocate behavior toward the latter?

That would create a possible feedback loop:

\[
\text{low estimated controllability}
\rightarrow
\text{ambitious engagement}\downarrow
\rightarrow
\text{reliable local reward}\uparrow
\rightarrow
\text{fewer efficacy-restoring experiences}\;?
\rightarrow
\text{low estimated controllability}.
\]

The "fewer efficacy-restoring experiences" edge is currently speculative and must be independently tested.

## Critical distinction: controller failure vs environment mismatch

One possibility worth preserving is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the controller can be locally sensible while the input environment is globally pathological for its assumptions}
}
\]

This differs from claiming either:

- the person is irrational;
- the environment is objectively hopeless;
- depressive illness is adaptive;
- withdrawal is generally beneficial.

A correctly functioning controller can still produce harmful outcomes when deployed outside the distribution under which its policy was useful.

## Testable predictions

A human version of the conjecture should predict more than a correlation with screen time.

Candidate variables include:

- perceived effort → reward coupling;
- perceived controllability;
- comparison-set size and extremity;
- perceived technological replaceability;
- volatility of occupational value;
- frequency of adverse comparison updates;
- availability of low-variance alternative rewards;
- withdrawal / exploration;
- recovery following restored controllability.

The strongest tests would manipulate **controllability and expected action-outcome coupling** rather than merely measuring internet exposure.

For example, holding expected value approximately fixed, does evidence that outcomes are controllable preserve exploration better than evidence that outcomes are exogenous?

Does credible evidence of restored efficacy reverse conservative behavior?

Do repeated unfavorable comparisons matter primarily when they imply low attainability or low control?

## What would count against this extension?

The extension weakens if:

- comparison, insecurity, and competition effects are unrelated to perceived controllability or action-outcome expectations;
- experimentally restoring controllability fails to affect withdrawal / exploration;
- chronic exposure does not differ qualitatively from episodic adverse input;
- reliable local rewards do not become more attractive as perceived efficacy declines;
- observed associations are better explained by unrelated mechanisms without any common control variable;
- the proposed variables fail to mediate or moderate relevant behavioral changes.

## Claim boundary

The literature currently supports several **pieces** of the proposed input environment.

It does not establish the synthesis:

\[
\text{modern technological environment}
\Rightarrow
\text{chronically activated loss-response controller}.
\]

That arrow is the research question.
